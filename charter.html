The charter · manifesto over marketing

Publish the cure.
Redact the recipe.

This fellowship imagines dangerous things on purpose. That only works if the danger stays on the page and the defense goes out the door. Here is exactly how we hold that line. Every fellow signs it.

01 · The redaction law

We do not publish operational hazard information. Full stop. No genetic sequences, no synthesis routes, no acquisition steps, no uplift methods, no "how the threat was built." A scenario names a failure mode; it never becomes a manual. When a project surfaces something genuinely hazardous, that material is documented once, sealed, and routed to the disclosure track — never to a public page.

The one-line test

Before anything ships, we ask one question: does this make a bad actor more capable? If yes, it does not publish — the fix ships, the hazard stays dark. If we can't make an artifact safe, we don't release it. The story can survive redaction. A person can't survive the alternative.

02 · Responsible disclosure

Every fix that touches a real vendor, tool, or gap runs through coordinated disclosure before any dispatch goes public:

  • Vendors first. Affected tool and device makers get the finding and a remediation window before publication.
  • Authorities in the loop. Genuinely hazardous detail goes to the appropriate biosecurity and public-health response channels — not to the internet.
  • Time to fix. We hold. Getting the defense adopted matters more than getting the story out fast.
  • A paper trail. Who was notified, what was withheld, what changed — the disclosure record is itself a fellowship deliverable.

03 · Dual-use review

An independent biosafety board — scientists, a security researcher, and an ethicist — reviews every project at two gates: after the red-team (before any bench work) and before the dispatch (before anything publishes). The board can redact, delay, or kill a release. Fellows don't overrule it, and neither do we. Its call is final.

04 · At the bench

All hands-on work happens in safety-reviewed community or institutional lab space under real BSL-1/2 discipline and local rules. Countermeasure demos use benign stand-ins — harmless, publicly-known material — never anything actually dangerous. We build detectors, screens, indicators, and drills. We do not build threats to "prove" they work. The threat's plausibility is established on paper, by the red-team, not on the bench.

05 · Code of conduct

The fellowship is a crew, and crews have rules. Every fellow, mentor, and staffer agrees to:

  • Treat everyone with respect. Harassment, discrimination, and intimidation get you removed — no debate.
  • Put safety and disclosure ahead of the story, the demo, the byline, and the clock. Always.
  • Follow the law and every hosting lab's rules. If a thing can't be done safely and legally, it doesn't get done.
  • Speak up. If something feels off — a scenario drifting toward a recipe, a demo cutting a corner — you stop the line. Raising a concern is protected; ignoring one isn't.
  • Credit the crew. Countermeasures ship open, with everyone who built them named.

06 · Why we work in the open at all

Because the alternative is worse. The threats we imagine are imaginable by anyone — that's the whole problem. Pretending otherwise doesn't make defenders ready; it makes them late. So we do the imagining out loud, in a controlled room, with the recipe under lock and the antidote on the table. That's the trade. We think it's the right one.

A monster on the page is a warning.
A monster with an antidote is a plan.

If the charter reads like common sense to you, you'll fit right in. If it reads like a constraint on the good stuff — this probably isn't your fellowship.
